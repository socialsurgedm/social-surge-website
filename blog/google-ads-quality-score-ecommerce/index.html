Google Ads Quality Score for E-Commerce: Why It Matters and How to Improve It

Quality Score is the hidden lever that determines whether you pay £0.60 or £1.80 for the same click. Here is what the score actually measures, why e-commerce accounts often struggle with it, and the structural fixes that genuinely move the needle.

By Jack Goldsmith, Founder & Performance Marketer, Social Surge · 1 October 2026

What is Google Ads Quality Score and does it matter for e-commerce?

Google Ads Quality Score is a 1-10 diagnostic metric assigned to each keyword. It is built from three components: Expected Click-Through Rate, Ad Relevance and Landing Page Experience. A higher score typically means lower CPCs and better ad positions for the same bid, making it a direct cost-efficiency lever for e-commerce stores running Search campaigns.

If your Google Ads costs keep rising without a clear cause, Quality Score is often where the answer sits. In the accounts we manage, stores with persistently high CPCs share a predictable structural problem: keywords, ads and landing pages that do not form a coherent, relevant chain. Quality Score is Google's way of measuring that chain, and it charges you more when the chain is broken.

What Google Ads Quality Score Actually Is (and What It Is Not)

Quality Score is a diagnostic number, not a live auction signal. It does not feed directly into your auction calculation. What determines ad position and the amount you pay per click is Ad Rank, which combines your bid with expected ad quality and several other contextual factors. Quality Score gives you a simplified 1-10 read on where your quality stands, updated based on recent account history.

Google is explicit about this distinction in their Quality Score Help documentation: treat it as a diagnostic tool, not a metric to obsess over directly. That said, improving the underlying components feeds into Ad Rank and reduces what you pay per click in practice, so it absolutely matters commercially.

The Three Components That Determine Your Score

Each keyword's Quality Score is assembled from three rated components, graded Below Average, Average or Above Average. Knowing which component is underperforming tells you exactly where to direct your effort.

Expected Click-Through Rate

Google predicts how likely your ad is to be clicked when shown for a given keyword, compared with other advertisers in the same auction. This prediction draws on your historical CTR for that keyword, adjusted for factors like ad position. It reflects how well your headlines speak to the search intent, not just how many impressions you accumulate.

Ad Relevance

This measures how closely your ad copy matches the keyword and the intent behind it. A generic ad served against a specific product keyword will typically score Below Average here. The root cause is almost always broad ad groups: too many unrelated keywords sharing one ad, so the copy cannot be specific enough for any of them.

Landing Page Experience

Google assesses whether your landing page is relevant to the keyword, easy to navigate, loads quickly and is transparent about the business behind it. For e-commerce this means the page reached after clicking on a "carbon road bike" keyword should be a carbon road bikes category or product page, not your homepage or a general cycling page. Page speed is a significant factor here; Google's PageSpeed guidance is directly applicable.

How Quality Score Affects Your CPCs and Ad Position

In the Google Ads auction, Ad Rank determines both where your ad appears and how much you pay per click. As Google explains, you pay the minimum needed to maintain your position, tied to the Ad Rank of the advertiser below you and your own quality. Higher quality means you need a lower bid to reach the same position, or you reach a better position for the same bid.

In practice, the difference between a Quality Score of 4 and 8 on the same keyword can typically mean paying 30-50% more per click for equivalent placement. For an e-commerce account spending £5,000 per month on Search, that gap can represent thousands of pounds in avoidable cost over a year. It is one of the first things we examine during a PPC audit.

Why E-Commerce Accounts Often Have Lower Quality Scores

E-commerce accounts carry more keywords, more product categories and more variation in landing pages than most lead-generation accounts. That breadth creates specific, recurring problems:

  • Overstuffed ad groups. Grouping "cycling shoes", "road cycling shoes" and "carbon cycling shoes" in one ad group means a single generic ad serves all three. Each individual keyword gets Below Average Ad Relevance because no single ad can be specific enough for all of them.
  • Homepage or category-page mismatch. Sending traffic for specific product searches to a top-level category page kills Landing Page Experience. Someone searching "Shimano GRX 810 groupset" expects to land on that product, not a general components page.
  • Low impression volume on long-tail keywords. Quality Score needs sufficient impression data to calculate accurately. New accounts and freshly added keywords will often show no score or a default number that does not reflect actual performance. This normalises with time.
  • Slow mobile pages. Most e-commerce traffic arrives on mobile. Unoptimised Shopify or WooCommerce themes with large images and render-blocking scripts drag Landing Page Experience scores across the whole account.

How to Improve Google Ads Quality Score for an E-Commerce Store

Rather than chasing the 1-10 number itself, fix the three underlying components in order of structural impact.

1. Restructure Into Tighter Ad Groups

The single biggest lever for Ad Relevance is reducing the keyword-to-ad ratio. Aim for ad groups covering a tight, coherent theme: one product type, one intent modifier. In a cycling store account, "road cycling shoes" belongs in its own ad group with dedicated headlines, separate from "MTB shoes" or "triathlon cycling shoes". Each group gets ad copy that uses the keyword phrase naturally and speaks to exactly what that searcher wants. This structural discipline underpins everything else in well-run Google Ads management.

2. Match Headlines to Search Intent

Expected CTR improves when your headline directly answers the searcher's specific need. For a product keyword, include the product name, a key differentiator (brand, specification, delivery promise) and a clear reason to click. "Carbon Road Cycling Shoes | Free UK Delivery | In Stock Now" will outperform "Cycling Shoes at Great Prices" for both CTR and Ad Relevance on a product-intent query.

3. Align Landing Pages to the Keyword

Map each ad group to the most specific landing page available. Product keyword groups should reach product pages or tightly filtered category URLs. If the right page does not exist, create a filtered URL using your platform's native faceted navigation rather than sending traffic somewhere generic. Then address page speed: run your core landing pages through PageSpeed Insights and treat images over 200 KB and render-blocking scripts as a first priority.

4. Use Negative Keywords to Protect CTR

Irrelevant impressions that attract no clicks drag Expected CTR down steadily. A thorough negative keyword strategy filters out the queries that will never convert, protecting the signal quality of the remaining traffic and reducing the wasted spend that compounds when budgets scale. What Not to Bother With

Several Quality Score "fixes" circulate widely but waste time or actively harm performance.

  • Increasing bids to improve Quality Score. Bids do not affect Quality Score. They feed Ad Rank directly, but the three quality components are calculated independently of your bid level.
  • Pausing and re-enabling keywords to reset scores. This discards accumulated performance history and typically makes things worse before they recover. Fix the underlying issue instead.
  • Chasing 10 out of 10 as an end goal. A 7 on a competitive product keyword that converts profitably is worth more to the business than a 10 on a term nobody buys from. Efficiency and profitability are the goal; Quality Score is a means to reach them.
  • Applying Search Quality Score logic to Shopping or Performance Max. Quality Score as a 1-10 figure applies only to Search campaigns. Shopping and Performance Max use different relevance signals, driven primarily by product feed quality. Our Shopping feed optimisation guide covers that separately, and it is a distinct track from Search quality work.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good Google Ads Quality Score for e-commerce?

For branded keywords, 8-10 is typical and realistic. For competitive product keywords, 6-7 is respectable. Scores of 4 or below on your core product terms usually signal a mismatch between keyword, ad copy and landing page that is worth fixing before you increase budget.

Does Quality Score directly affect ROAS?

Not directly, but it affects the CPC you pay for a given ad position. A higher Quality Score means you can reach the same position at a lower cost, which reduces your cost per click and, if conversion rate stays constant, improves ROAS. Think of it as a cost-efficiency lever rather than a direct ROAS dial.

How long does it take to improve Quality Score?

Quality Score is a rolling calculation based on recent performance, so changes can start to show within a few weeks after meaningful account edits. However, Expected CTR in particular takes time to accumulate enough impression data. Structural fixes like tighter ad groups and better landing pages tend to move Ad Relevance and Landing Page Experience faster than CTR shifts.

Should I pause low Quality Score keywords?

Not automatically. A low Quality Score on a keyword that converts profitably is still worth running; fix the underlying issues rather than pausing it. Pause keywords that are low Quality Score AND low conversion rate AND high cost, because those three together mean you are paying over the odds for traffic that does not buy.
