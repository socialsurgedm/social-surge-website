How to Create a Paid Ad Campaign That Rapidly Increases Your Online Store's Sales

Fix your tracking first, choose the right platform for your stage, give the algorithm room to learn. Here's the step-by-step process — with realistic budgets and honest timelines.

By Jack Goldsmith — Founder & Performance Marketer, Social Surge · 16 July 2026

How do I create a paid ad campaign to rapidly increase my online store's sales?

Start by fixing your conversion tracking — Google Ads purchase events with revenue values, Meta Pixel plus Conversions API. Then launch Google Shopping or a tightly scoped Performance Max campaign on your best-selling products with at least £30–50/day. Add Meta prospecting once you have 50–100 purchases to build audience signals from. Keep your campaign structure simple, your negative keyword list active from week one, and give the algorithm 60–90 days before drawing conclusions. The stores that scale fastest are not the ones with the biggest budgets — they're the ones with the cleanest data and the discipline to leave campaigns alone while they learn.

Creating a paid ad campaign that rapidly increases your online store's sales depends on three prerequisites: your tracking must be reliable, your first campaign must be narrow enough to generate real signal, and your budget must be large enough to exit the learning phase. Miss any one of these and you are spending money on data you cannot trust or running campaigns that never stabilise. This guide covers the exact setup process we use when onboarding a new e-commerce client — the platform order, the structural logic, the budget thresholds, and what to realistically expect in the first quarter.

Fix Your Conversion Tracking Before Spending a Single Pound

Every paid advertising platform — Google Ads, Meta, everywhere — makes budget and bidding decisions based on the conversion signals you feed it. If those signals are wrong, every pound you spend is training the algorithm in the wrong direction. In the accounts we manage, the first task on any new account is a thorough tracking audit, before a single campaign goes live.

Specifically, check three things:

  • Google Ads conversion tracking: your primary conversion action must be "Purchase" with transaction-level revenue values (not a flat £1 or nothing). Use Google's Tag Assistant to verify that the tag fires on your order-confirmation page and that the value matches actual order totals.
  • Meta Pixel + Conversions API: browser-only pixels routinely undercount conversions by 20–40% on iOS devices and Safari due to cookie restrictions. Server-side Conversions API (CAPI) closes that gap significantly. If you are not running CAPI, your Meta reporting is flattering you and your audience signals are degraded.
  • GA4 purchase events: confirm that transaction_id, value and currency parameters are present. Cross-reference GA4 purchase totals against your order management system weekly during the first month — discrepancies above 10% typically indicate a tag firing issue or duplicate events.

A proper tracking setup takes a day to configure correctly. It prevents months of misreading data and making budget decisions on numbers that were never accurate. Our guide on Google Ads management covers the conversion setup we implement as standard.

Which Platform Should You Start On — Google or Meta?

For most UK e-commerce stores, the right sequence is Google first, Meta second. The reason is demand versus attention. Google Shopping and Search intercept buyers who are already searching for your product — you are capturing intent that exists today. Meta reaches people who match your customer profile but are not actively searching — it creates demand rather than capturing it, which is a harder commercial equation at launch.

The practical order of operations:

  • Step 1 — Google Shopping or Performance Max: your first campaign. Targets buyers at the bottom of the funnel who know what they want. Typically delivers the strongest early ROAS because you're not persuading anyone, you're just being visible when the intent is highest.
  • Step 2 — Google Brand Search: add only if you have meaningful brand search volume. Running brand campaigns before people know you exist wastes budget capturing organic clicks you would have received anyway.
  • Step 3 — Meta Ads prospecting: add once you have 50–100 purchase conversions tracked. Below this threshold, Meta's lookalike audiences have too little signal to be useful and broad cold audiences convert at CPAs that do not make commercial sense for most stores.

The exception: if your products are genuinely visual and impulse-led — fashion, gifts, homeware — Meta can run earlier because the creative does the heavy selling. But in that case, watch CPA tightly and set a hard stop-loss if week-two CPA is more than twice your target.

How to Structure Your First Google Shopping or PMax Campaign

The most common structural mistake is a single campaign containing your entire product catalogue. It means bestsellers and slow-movers compete for the same daily budget, and Google's algorithm — left to its own preferences — frequently spends on lower-value SKUs that generate cheaper conversions rather than higher-revenue ones.

A cleaner starting structure, which we use on most new e-commerce accounts:

  • Campaign 1 — Hero Products: your 10–20 best-converting SKUs, or your single most commercially important product category. Allocate 60–70% of your Google Ads budget here. Whether you're running Standard Shopping or Performance Max, keep the scope tight so the algorithm optimises on meaningful conversion volume rather than noise.
  • Campaign 2 — Broader Catalogue: remaining products at a lower priority or budget cap. This catches long-tail searches without cannibalising the hero campaign's spend.
  • Shared negative keyword list: build this from day one and apply it across both campaigns. Branded competitor terms, irrelevant product queries, informational searches — these drain budget silently on broad-match setups and PMax's automatically generated asset variants.

For Performance Max specifically: keep asset groups themed by product category, not mixed. Give the campaign at least 3–4 weeks before reading performance data — PMax's learning phase is real, and making structural changes before 30 days routinely resets progress. How to Structure Your First Meta Ads Campaign

Meta's campaign structure maps to funnel stages, and keeping each stage in its own campaign with its own budget is critical. If you combine prospecting and retargeting in the same campaign, Meta will typically favour retargeting conversions — they are cheaper and easier to attribute — and your warm audience pool (often just a few hundred people) will absorb a disproportionate share of your prospecting budget.

  • Prospecting campaign: broad or interest-based audiences, Sales objective, new customers only. Run 2–3 creative variations per ad set: a static product image, a short video or Reel, and a dynamic product catalogue carousel. Creative quality drives performance far more than audience targeting on Meta's current algorithm.
  • Retargeting campaign: 7–30 day website visitors, 7–14 day add-to-cart and checkout-initiated events who did not purchase. Smaller daily budgets — £10–20/day depending on traffic volume — but typically very high intent and the best early ROAS signal.
  • Existing customers excluded from prospecting via a customer list exclusion. Do not show acquisition ads to people who already bought from you.

What Budget Do You Actually Need?

The budget question has an honest answer that most agencies avoid because it is not always what potential clients want to hear. Here are the realistic minimums for generating enough conversion data to make decisions — not guarantees of profitable results, but the floor required for a fair test:

  • Google Shopping or PMax: £30–50/day (typically £900–£1,500/month) to exit the learning phase within 30 days at standard UK e-commerce CPCs. Below this, you may generate only 5–10 conversions per month — not enough for the algorithm to optimise bid strategies or for you to identify which products are working.
  • Meta Ads prospecting: £20–30/day (£600–£900/month). Below this, audience sizes become constrained and Meta's delivery system cannot optimise ad placement effectively.
  • Meta Ads retargeting: £10–15/day if your site is under 5,000 monthly visitors. Scale proportionally with traffic — retargeting pools must be large enough to sustain campaign delivery.

Running both channels simultaneously, a realistic launch budget is £1,500–£2,500/month. Below this, you are typically not generating enough weekly conversions to draw conclusions, and decisions end up being made on statistically meaningless data. Published pricing for our management services reflects this reality — we do not onboard clients whose budgets are too small to work with, because it is not commercially honest to take a management fee on a spend level that cannot succeed.

The 60-90 Day Ramp — What to Realistically Expect

Month one is disproportionately expensive relative to revenue generated. Campaigns are in learning. You are building negative keyword lists from scratch. Tracking is being validated against real orders. Expect ROAS to sit well below your long-term target and expect to make structural adjustments — not panic changes, but measured ones based on search term reports and audience segment performance.

Month two is typically when campaigns stabilise. Patterns emerge: which products, which ad groups, which audiences, which days of the week produce your best CPA. This is when budget decisions become data-backed rather than speculative. In the accounts we manage, month two is usually when we make our first meaningful scaling moves.

Month three and beyond is when scaling logic kicks in properly: increase budgets on what is clearly working, restructure or pause what is not, add brand search if brand recall has built up from prospecting activity. Our niche e-commerce accounts typically hit their target ROAS range in months two to three and maintain or improve it from there — in line with our published results.

The biggest predictor of whether a paid ad campaign succeeds is not budget size — it is whether the store owner has the discipline to let campaigns run through the learning phase without making structural changes every fortnight. Constant restructuring means you are always in week one.

Campaign Mistakes That Kill Early Momentum

A short list of what we see most commonly on new audit calls — each of these is avoidable:

  • No conversion value in tracking: the algorithm optimises for conversions, not revenue. Without value data, it will find the cheapest £9.99 orders if you sell across a wide price range — technically efficient, commercially pointless.
  • Brand campaigns without brand search volume: running brand terms before anyone knows your brand exists wastes budget on conversions that would have happened organically.
  • Broad match keywords without a managed negative list: budget disappears on irrelevant queries within days. Build your negative list before you launch, not after you see the search terms report.
  • Pausing campaigns overnight or at weekends: this creates artificial budget fluctuations and repeatedly resets the learning phase. Use bid adjustments or dayparting for scheduling rather than hard pauses.
  • Judging Meta on last-click ROAS only: Meta frequently assists conversions that other channels capture the last click on. Always check GA4 assisted conversions and view-through attribution before concluding Meta is not working.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I spend to test paid ads for my online store?

A realistic floor for generating meaningful data is £1,500–£2,500 per month across both Google and Meta. Below £30/day on Google Shopping or PMax, campaigns rarely exit the learning phase before you run out of patience. On Meta, less than £20/day limits audience sizes so severely that the algorithm cannot optimise. These are not guarantees of profitable results — they are the minimums required to make a fair test.

How long before my first paid ad campaign generates sales?

Some stores see their first sale within days; that does not mean the campaign is working at scale. Month one is the learning phase — campaigns are expensive relative to revenue generated. Month two is when patterns stabilise. Month three is typically when you have enough data to make confident scaling decisions. Stores that expect profitable ROAS by day 14 and pull the plug are the ones that never find out whether it would have worked.

Should I start with Google Ads or Meta Ads for my online store?

For most UK e-commerce stores, start with Google Shopping or Performance Max. You are capturing buyers who are already searching for your product — demand that exists today. Meta Ads create demand by reaching people who match your customer profile but are not actively searching. Add Meta prospecting once you have 50–100 purchases to build audience signals from; cold Meta audiences with no purchase data convert poorly and waste budget early. If your products are visually led or impulse-buy, Meta can run earlier — but watch your CPA tightly.

What is the most common reason a paid ad campaign fails for online stores?

Broken or incomplete conversion tracking. If your purchase events are not firing with transaction-level revenue values, every platform optimises for the wrong signal — cheap clicks or low-value orders rather than real revenue. The second most common failure is restructuring campaigns too frequently. Constant changes reset the learning phase and mean you are always in week one. Set up tracking correctly, launch a focused structure, then give it 60 days before drawing conclusions.
